Credit Scores in Plain English

Credit scores show up everywhere. Lenders mention them. Landlords ask about them. Some employers even glance at them. But most people never get a clear, honest explanation of what the number means, how it gets built, or why it matters more in some moments than others.

Here is the version nobody gives you.

What a Credit Score Actually Measures

A credit score is a shortcut. Lenders use it to guess how likely you are to pay back money you borrow. That is the whole purpose. It does not measure your wealth. It does not reflect your income. It does not tell anyone whether you are responsible with money in general. It only tells them how you have handled borrowed money in the past.

The most commonly used version is the FICO score, which ranges from 300 to 850. FICO stands for Fair Isaac Corporation, the company that created the scoring model. Higher is better. Most lenders consider anything above about 670 to be solid. Above 740 is excellent. Below 600, and you start running into higher interest rates, fewer options, and more headaches.

This is where the names can get confusing. FICO is not the same thing as Experian, Equifax, or TransUnion. Those three are the major credit bureaus. They collect the information in your credit report: accounts, balances, payment history, late payments, collections, and so on. FICO then uses that kind of information to calculate a score.

There are also newer scoring models, like VantageScore. VantageScore is often what you see in free services like Credit Karma. It is useful for tracking general movement, but it may not be the exact score a mortgage lender or car lender uses. For those decisions, FICO is still the big one.

Then there are credit monitoring and identity-protection services. Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion offer their own monitoring tools. myFICO focuses on showing actual FICO scores. Credit Karma is useful for free score tracking and report alerts. LifeLock and IdentityIQ lean more toward identity theft protection, fraud alerts, and monitoring across different accounts. They are all related to credit, but they do not all do the same job.

The Five Ingredients

FICO calculates your score using five factors. Understanding them is not complicated, and knowing the weight of each one helps you prioritize.

Payment history is the biggest piece. It makes up about 35 percent of your score. The rule is simple: pay on time, every time. Even one late payment can cause real damage, and that damage lingers for years. The best strategy is boring but effective. Set up automatic minimum payments so you never accidentally miss one, then pay the full balance manually when you can.

Credit utilization is the second biggest factor. This is the ratio of how much credit you are using compared to how much you have available. If you have a credit card with a $10,000 limit and you carry a $7,000 balance, you are using 70 percent of your available credit, and that looks risky. Most experts recommend keeping utilization below 30 percent. Some suggest below 10 percent for the best scores. This factor updates monthly, so paying down a balance can improve your score relatively quickly.

Length of credit history matters too. About 15 percent of your score depends on how long your accounts have been open. This is why closing an old credit card can sometimes hurt your score, even if you never use it. The account's age and history disappear from the calculation, and your average account age drops.

Credit mix contributes about 10 percent. Lenders like to see that you can handle different types of credit: a credit card, a car loan, maybe a mortgage. You do not need all of them, and you should never take on debt just to improve this factor, but having some variety helps.

New credit inquiries make up the final 10 percent. Every time you apply for new credit, a hard inquiry appears on your report. One here and there is not a problem. But several in a short period can signal desperation, and that pulls your score down.

What Does Not Affect Your Score

This part surprises people. Your salary does not affect your score. Your bank balance does not either. Rent payments are generally not reported to credit bureaus unless you are in a special program. Utilities, subscriptions, and debit card usage have no direct impact.

Your score is based on how you handle borrowed money, not how you handle the money you already have. That distinction matters because it explains why some people with high incomes have mediocre scores and some people with modest incomes have excellent ones.

How to Build or Fix One

If you are starting from nothing, a secured credit card is often the simplest entry point. You put down a small deposit, usually a few hundred dollars, and that becomes your credit limit. Use it for small purchases, pay it off every month, and let the history build. After a year or so, many issuers will upgrade you to a regular card.

If your score has taken a hit, the first step is to pull your credit report. You can get it free once a year from each of the three major bureaus through AnnualCreditReport.com. Check for errors. They are more common than people think, and a single mistake can drag your score down for no good reason.

Disputing errors is slower than anyone would like, but it is worth the effort. Inaccurate information is one of the few things you can actually remove from your report.

If the damage is real, not a mistake, the fix is patience and consistency. Bring any past-due accounts current. Keep balances low. Do not close old accounts unless they carry fees you cannot avoid. Time heals most credit damage, but only if your recent behavior is better than your older history.

Why This Matters Beyond Borrowing

A good credit score does more than get you lower interest rates. It gives you options. When your score is solid, you can qualify for better apartments, lower insurance premiums in some states, and more flexible terms on the things you need to finance.

When your score is weak, everything costs more. Not just loans. Rent deposits get bigger. Insurance rates climb. Some employers hesitate. The number follows you into rooms you did not expect.

That is why it is worth understanding. Not because the system is fair. It is not always. But because knowing how it works gives you a small amount of power in a system that otherwise operates quietly behind the scenes.
